Shopping — what is on screen when you open it

Four directions for the shopping plugin's opening surface, each rendered in the real primitives — ListRow (hairline, 56 px floor, leading slot), ItemCard, PageContainer, the Olive & Teal tokens and the mobile shell chrome. Nothing here is described in prose that another direction gets drawn for.

The axis

What is the primary object on screen when you open Shopping — and what does the app think you are about to do?

The answer already rejected is “you are about to choose a list.” PR #127 shipped an index of lists with a drill-in and it read as filing, not shopping. The four directions below answer the question as add · shop · see · go.

A constraint the brief did not carry — found while reading the shell

The mobile bottom tab bar is capped at 5 tabs (ADR 033, amended 2026-08-17): Home + the first three tier-visible plugins + More, pinned in plugins.json registry order. That order today is calendar · chores · meals · rewards · family-feed. A sixth plugin lands inside the More sheet — two taps and a sheet.

Every direction below assumes Shopping is pinned into the four, because capture speed is the acceptance bar and a two-tap capture loses to Google Keep before the surface design gets a vote. That pin costs another plugin its tab — on the phone, meals is the weakest claim (it is a planning surface, opened weekly, not a standing-in-the-kitchen surface). This is a decision this session should hand to M5.5/2 explicitly, not leave to registry order.

Direction A — The line is the page

Capture-first

You are about to add something. The list is what your addition falls into.

The capture field is the hero and it is the first thing under the page title — a 56 px field with the destination as a chip on its trailing edge. Type, hit enter, the word files itself and the field stays open for the next one. The aisle-grouped list runs underneath and scrolls the field away when you are actually shopping. One surface, one posture: the app is always ready to be typed into.

What it costs. The field owns the top of a screen that is also the screen you hold in an aisle — and mid-shop it is dead weight you have to scroll past before the first row. A3 solves that by letting it scroll away, which means the in-shop state is the same screen minus its hero: no wrong mode, but also no posture. Store switching hides in a chip menu, so a Costco list is a two-tap discovery rather than a visible thing.

What it buys. The fastest possible add — the field is under your thumb the instant the route paints, with no tap to open it. It is the only direction that beats Keep on its own terms.

Direction B — You are already in the list

Shop-first

You are about to shop. The app picks the list and drops you into it, mid-walk.

There is no index and no chooser: opening Shopping lands you in Grocery, aisle-grouped in walk order, with a progress track under the title and every row already checkable. The other destinations are a pill strip that swaps the list in place — a filter, not a drill-in. Adding is a pinned bar above the tab bar, always reachable by thumb, never competing with the list for the top of the screen.

What it costs. It opens in shopping posture on the ~90 % of opens that are somebody adding one thing at the fridge. The add bar is thumb-reachable but still one tap from a keyboard, so it is measurably slower than A. And the pill strip is a soft index: three pills is fine, seven is the rejected shape wearing a costume.

What it buys. The list is the page — nothing above it but its own progress. Store switching is visible rather than discovered, and “14 to get, 6 in the cart” is legible from the top of the screen without reading a single row.

Direction C — Everything the house needs, in one stream

Needs-first

You are about to see what the house needs. Where it gets bought is a property of the thing, not a place you go.

One list, no destinations at the top. Every outstanding item in the house sits in one section-grouped stream, and the store is a small tag on the row that carries one. When you are actually somewhere, you say so — “I'm at Grocery” — and the stream narrows to what you can buy there and turns into the shopping surface. The model still has lists; the surface refuses to make you choose one.

What it costs — and C3 is drawn so the cost is visible, not argued. Aisle order is walk order in one shop (ADR 069 §5). Merge three shops into one stream and the grouping stops meaning anything: Frozen holds a Costco salmon you cannot buy at the grocery store, Fasteners sits between Pantry and Other, and the reader has to filter by tag with their eyes. The narrowing in C2 is therefore not a convenience — it is mandatory, which means the default screen is the one nobody ever shops from.

What it buys. It is the only direction that answers “do we need anything?” in one glance without picking a context first, and the only one where adding never requires knowing which store a thing comes from.

Direction D — The stream at rest, the run when you go

Trip-first

You are about to drop something into the family's stream — until someone says they're going, and then the app becomes a trip.

At rest there is no list and no chooser: there is what the house has been adding, newest first, with who and when — the shape the family already has in Keep. One primary band sits above it: Going out? Tapping it names the store and the surface changes into a live run — aisle order, progress, whole-row checks, and everyone at home watching it fill and adding into it in real time. Finishing the run ends it. Two surfaces, and the mode is bound to a real-world event rather than a toggle.

What it costs. A run is ceremony: one tap and a store choice stand between you and a shoppable screen, and a person who forgets to start one is shopping from a reverse-chronological log, which is the worst possible order in an aisle. Reverse-chron is also bad at the one question a shared list gets asked constantly — “did someone already put milk on here?” — because the same word can sit three days apart in two places. D3 leans hard on identity, which ADR 064 says shopping does not get: “Dad is at Grocery” is a person as the answer, so a spine is defensible there and nowhere else in the module.

What it buys. The only direction where the house can see a trip happening and act on it — the “I'm at the store, anything else?” text message, built in. And the only one whose two modes are justified by something real rather than a control.

The case for W2. Nobody shops from a wall-mounted tablet. Everything the wall is physically good at is capture — you are standing at the fridge with the empty carton in your hand — and everything it is bad at is the aisle. W2 makes the wall one enormous input with a short memory, and hands the list itself to the phone.

A defect both inherit. The aisle labels above are the real section-label token — 11 px uppercase — and at six feet they are unreadable, while the rows beside them clear the 18 px across-the-room floor. The distance rule (design-system §Typography) sets a floor for primary content and says nothing about structural labels, so the wall has no label size that clears the distance. On a surface whose legibility depends entirely on aisle grouping, that is a hole in the scale, not a styling choice. Whichever wall answer wins owes a decision here — a distance variant of section-label, or aisle names promoted to heading on the wall.

The case for W1. One surface, one mental model, no second thing to design, build, and keep in sync — and the two-column aisle layout is genuinely useful for the “are we out of anything?” glance while cooking. W2 is a second surface that M5.5/4 has to build and that every later change has to touch twice.

A's capture field is the hero; B's list is the page. The field sits under the title with the destination as a chip on its trailing edge; the aisle-ordered list runs beneath it and scrolls the field away when you are in a shop. The destination pill strip is visible, and switching it swaps the list in place — never a drill-in. There is no mode: checking is live at rest, adding is live mid-shop, and progress reads from the top of the screen without reading a row.

What the hybrid gives up, stated so nobody re-discovers it as a bug. There is no live run (D) — the house cannot see that someone is at the store, and “anything else while I'm here?” stays a text message. There is no whole-house glance (C) — answering “do we need anything anywhere?” means reading three pills, not one list. Both are additive later; neither is designed around here.

The one thing the hybrid must not do. The pill strip is a filter that swaps the list in place. The moment it becomes a screen you land on, or grows past about five destinations, it is the index-of-lists that #127 was rejected for. If a sixth destination ever appears, that is a design question, not a scroll affordance.

What the gate answered

Direction: A on B — the hybrid drawn above. The wall: W2 — it only takes things in. The tab: Shopping is pinned into the four and Chores moves into More, so the phone reads Home · Calendar · Meals · Shopping · More. The contract M5.5/2 builds from is docs/modules/shopping/design.md; this artifact is its picture, and the two are read together.
